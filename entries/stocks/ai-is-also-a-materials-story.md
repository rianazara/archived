The chain the post draws: more AI → more data centres → more hardware →
more minerals. Every data centre is a physical asset before it's a compute
asset.

Where the materials go:

- **Copper** — power delivery and data cabling throughout the facility.
- **Gold and silver** — chip packaging and connectors, for conductivity and
  reliability at scale.
- **Aluminium** — heat sinks and cooling, as compute density rises.
- **Specialty metals** (gallium, germanium, tantalum) — high-performance
  semiconductors and high-speed data transfer.
- **Rare earths** — high-performance magnets in hard drives, which still
  store the bulk of large datasets.

Why it's a genuinely different lens: the compute layers in
[[ai-infrastructure-stack]] all depend on inputs with long lead times and
concentrated geography. Mine and refinery capacity doesn't scale on a
product cycle, so materials can become the binding constraint even when
chip capacity exists.

The closing rhetorical question — miners or gold — is doing persuasion
rather than analysis. Commodity exposure brings its own problems that the
post skips entirely: price cyclicality, geopolitical supply risk, and
companies whose fortunes track a commodity rather than the AI demand being
described.
