The claim as made: most people keep a goal in thought alone, and writing it,
reading it, saying it aloud and visualizing it each engage something
different — after which "your subconscious has no choice."

**What's actually defensible.** Each of those is a separate encoding route,
and multiple routes genuinely improve recall — writing by hand forces
you to compress and commit to specific words, saying it aloud adds
articulation, and picturing it forces concrete detail where an abstract
intention can stay comfortably vague. Writing goals down also makes them
reviewable, which is most of why written goals outperform held ones.

**What isn't.** There's no mechanism by which repetition across modes
obliges a subconscious to deliver an outcome, and "manifestation" framing
quietly replaces planning with rehearsal. Encoding a goal more thoroughly
makes you more likely to *notice opportunities and act*; it doesn't act for
you.

The usable version: write the goal in specific words, say it to someone who
will ask about it, and describe concretely what doing it looks like — then
treat that as the start of a plan rather than the plan.
