**What an FCNR deposit is.** An NRI deposits foreign currency with an Indian
bank and is repaid in that same currency — so there's no rupee exchange
risk on the principal. Interest is tax-free in India, though what you owe
depends entirely on your country of residence.

**Why the rate moved.** The bank converts those dollars to rupees to lend
out, and must convert back at maturity, so it hedges the currency exposure —
costing it roughly 3.5%. One saved post explains that the RBI agreed to
absorb that hedging cost to attract foreign capital, letting banks pass the
saving through as higher deposit rates.

**The leveraged version**, from the second post, is where this stops being a
deposit. The structure: put in some capital, borrow a multiple of it from an
offshore bank, with the Indian bank issuing a standby letter of credit as
the guarantee. You earn the spread between the deposit rate and the
borrowing cost on the whole amount. At 9× leverage with a 2% spread, the
arithmetic produces a headline return several times the unlevered one.

**Why the arithmetic is the least interesting part.** The post's own caveat
is the real content: this works only while the deposit rate stays
comfortably above borrowing cost. It shows that a rise in borrowing cost
from 5% to 6.5% cuts the headline 25% to roughly 11.5% — and the same
mechanism continues past zero. Leverage multiplies losses identically to
gains, a standby letter of credit is a liability to the issuing bank, and
spread-compression trades are a recurring way sophisticated people lose
capital.

Also: both posts hinge on an RBI window stated to close on 30 September,
which has passed — so the specific rates are historical.
